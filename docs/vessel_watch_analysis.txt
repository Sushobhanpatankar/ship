Current analysis of India's energy import landscape reveals eight vessels at various Indian ports: seven crude tankers and one LNG carrier. No CNG vessels are present. The crude tankers are distributed across Paradip (four berthed), Mundra (one berthed, one anchored), and Kochi (one anchored), while the single LNG carrier is anchored at Kochi. A critical caveat for this briefing is the highly stale nature of the AIS data, captured almost four days ago on June 3rd, rendering the real-time accuracy of this snapshot severely compromised.

The observed cargo breakdown heavily favors crude oil, with a 7:1 ratio against LNG. This snapshot doesn't indicate any immediate shift from India's typical energy import reliance on crude. Given the presence of vessels at west coast ports like Kochi and Mundra, the crude is likely sourced predominantly from the Middle East, with potential contributions from Russia or Africa. The LNG carrier at Kochi typically points to origins from the Middle East, primarily Qatar. For Paradip on the east coast, crude supply also largely originates from the Middle East, often transiting the Indian Ocean, or from Russian flows via the Suez Canal or around Africa.

A noteworthy pattern is the concentration of four crude tankers actively berthed at Paradip, suggesting significant discharge operations at that facility. The remaining crude vessels at Mundra and Kochi, along with the LNG carrier at Kochi, are either anchored or berthed. However, without live AIS updates, their current operational status and exact timelines for discharge or departure cannot be accurately determined. The complete absence of any recorded inbound or outbound vessels in this dataset is highly unusual for a major importer like India, further highlighting significant data sparsity.

The most significant analytical hurdle is the complete lack of any reported inbound vessels to India. This means no current sea lanes are observable for active traffic, and consequently, no short-term outlook for new arrivals in the next 12-24 hours can be derived from this data. This extreme data gap, coupled with the four-day old AIS information, means that any conclusions drawn regarding current supply origins, traffic patterns, or immediate future arrivals are speculative and based on typical rather than current real-time observations. It is highly probable that the actual vessel movements and port activities in India are substantially different from what this outdated and incomplete snapshot portrays.
